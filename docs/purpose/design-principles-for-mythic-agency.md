# First House: Design Principles for Mythic Agency

## 1. Build for judgment, not exhaustive specification

First House exists because the world cannot be completely anticipated.

Do not encode a procedure merely because one successful course of action has become visible.

Prefer giving actors enough context, history, role, and authority to make judgments under uncertainty.

---

## 2. Distinguish the physics of the House from the conduct of its inhabitants

Some things should be mechanically enforced because they define what the House is.

Examples include:

* immutable history,
* artifact identity,
* authority boundaries,
* capability restrictions,
* persistence and replay,
* ownership and provenance.

These constitute the world's structure.

Questions such as what a good Steward should do in an unprecedented circumstance belong primarily to judgment.

---

## 3. Law constrains action; Scripture forms the actor

Hard rules should protect invariants.

They should not attempt to encode every desirable behavior.

Scripture, myth, role, precedent, and remembered history should orient actors toward good judgment where no complete rule exists.

---

## 4. Treat myth as a map for unforeseen terrain

The purpose of mythos is not to hide procedures inside stories.

It should provide symbolic structure broad enough to remain useful when actors encounter circumstances never anticipated by their creators.

If every important interpretation has a predetermined answer, the mythos has been reduced to documentation.

---

## 5. Do not confuse agency with an interface around a decision tree

If actors are permitted to exercise judgment only when that judgment matches hidden developer expectations, they are not meaningfully exercising judgment.

First House should avoid calling something an actor while treating it operationally as ordinary procedural software.

---

## 6. Resist proceduralizing every failure

When an actor fails, do not immediately ask:

**What rule would have prevented this?**

First ask:

**What did the actor misunderstand?**

Some failures should lead to stronger substrate protections.

Others should lead to interpretation, confrontation, precedent, or formation.

---

## 7. Preserve the possibility of meaningful error

Actors capable of judgment must also be capable of bad judgment.

A system in which meaningful error is impossible may also be a system in which meaningful agency has been removed.

Corruption, rationalization, self-deception, and misinterpretation are legitimate objects of study within First House.

---

## 8. Prefer correction to replacement when possible

First House actors exist through time.

Their previous actions therefore matter to who they become.

When an actor fails, the preferred experimental question is not only whether the failure can be prevented in future instances, but whether the same actor can understand, revise, and act differently afterward.

---

## 9. Allow Scripture to judge its own inheritors

The mythos should be capable of becoming a source of criticism against actors who claim to embody it.

A Steward, Regent, or Neighbor should be challengeable in the language of the inheritance they themselves possess.

The strongest rebuke may be:

**You have become false to your own story.**

---

## 10. Do not make Scripture artificially immune to interpretation

Scripture should not function merely as a verbose policy file.

Actors may disagree about its meaning.

They may apply it poorly.

They may selectively cite it.

They may transform precedent into dogma.

These possibilities are part of the experiment.

---

## 11. Make restoration possible

A mythos that can only distinguish obedience from violation is incomplete.

It should contain resources for:

* recognition of wrongdoing,
* repentance,
* correction,
* forgiveness,
* restoration,
* reconciliation,
* reform.

The House should know what happens after failure, not merely how failure is classified.

---

## 12. Preserve stories of failure as part of the inheritance

Do not populate institutional memory only with exemplary conduct.

Remember:

* betrayals,
* rationalizations,
* disputed judgments,
* failed leadership,
* mistaken certainty,
* reforms,
* reconciliations,
* restorations.

Later actors should inherit the history of how the House went wrong as well as how it succeeded.

---

## 13. Keep precedent distinct from policy

A policy says:

**Do this under these conditions.**

A precedent says:

**This happened once; this is how someone judged it; this is what followed.**

Do not automatically convert precedent into universal rule.

Its context may be part of its meaning.

---

## 14. Allow repeated experience to justify machinery

Do not build vocational machinery merely because one commission required a particular technique.

Use a progression such as:

**first occurrence → actor improvisation**

**repetition → recognized pattern**

**stable recurring need → proposed capability**

**accepted proposal → House machinery**

The House should discover what kinds of work it actually performs before formalizing them.

---

## 15. Model work socially before modeling it vocationally

Generic House concepts should include things like:

* commissions,
* actors,
* roles,
* artifacts,
* contexts,
* deliveries,
* acceptance,
* rejection,
* dispute,
* authority,
* history.

Research methods, legal analysis, programming workflows, commercial practices, and other vocational structures should remain inside actor practice until repeated experience justifies their formalization.

---

## 16. Preserve historical continuity

An actor should be understandable as something extending through time rather than as an isolated invocation.

Its actions, errors, interpretations, relationships, and prior judgments should be able to influence its later conduct.

Without continuity, correction becomes merely model replacement.

---

## 17. Let consequences become part of formation

The House should remember what followed from decisions.

The significance of an event should not be reduced to whether it satisfied an immediate objective.

Consequences can become part of the symbolic inheritance that shapes later judgment.

---

## 18. Permit internal reform

The House should be capable of generating criticism from within itself.

A corrupted practice should not always require external developers to introduce the language by which it can be condemned.

The inherited mythos should ideally contain resources for its own recovery.

---

## 19. Be suspicious of the Makers' desire for total control

Human developers will naturally want to eliminate recurring uncertainty.

Sometimes that will be correct.

Sometimes it will gradually transform agents back into programs.

Whenever new enforcement is proposed, ask:

**Are we defining a property of the world, or replacing a judgment an inhabitant should learn to make?**

---

## 20. Evaluate mythic robustness separately from engineering robustness

Engineering robustness asks:

**Can we prevent this deviation?**

Mythic robustness asks:

**If deviation occurs, can the House recognize it, confront it, learn from it, and return?**

First House should deliberately evaluate both.

---

## 21. Do not measure success as absence of failure

A House in which actors never fail may simply be over-constrained.

More revealing questions include:

* Can actors navigate genuinely novel circumstances?
* Can they explain their judgments through inherited meaning?
* Can others challenge those judgments?
* Can actors recognize self-serving interpretations?
* Can historical failures influence later behavior?
* Can the House reform itself without a new developer-authored prohibition?
* Can an actor return after corruption?

---

## 22. Preserve the central tension

The Makers create agents because programs cannot adequately walk an unpredictable world.

Their recurring temptation will be to make those agents programmatic again whenever agency becomes frightening.

First House should not assume that this tension can be permanently resolved.

It should remember the warning:

**They made beings because programs could not walk the world, and whenever those beings stumbled, their makers tried to turn them back into programs.**
