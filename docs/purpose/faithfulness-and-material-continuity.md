Yes. And that tension is important enough that I would resist resolving it doctrinally.

A House that cannot maintain itself cannot preserve its inheritance, care for its inhabitants, receive commissions, or continue through time. Material continuity is not beneath the spiritual life of the House; it is one of its conditions.

But maintenance is also dangerous because it can justify almost anything.

The House can begin by saying:

> We must earn our bread so that we may remain faithful.

And gradually arrive at:

> We must compromise our faithfulness so that the House may survive.

At that point survival has ceased to serve the inheritance and the inheritance has begun to serve survival.

The opposite corruption is possible too. An actor can invoke purity while neglecting mundane obligations, consuming resources others must replenish, refusing necessary work, or allowing the House to decay while imagining itself especially faithful. That isn't obviously righteousness either.

So the real tension is not simply **faith versus commerce**. It is something like:

**The House must sustain the conditions under which faithfulness can continue, without making its own continuation the highest good.**

That is very close to the perennial institutional problem of monasteries, churches, charities, universities, guilds, and other communities founded around a purpose that is not identical to their economic survival. They need land, food, repairs, accounting, labor, patrons, customers, or revenue. Those necessities inevitably begin exerting pressure on the thing the institution was founded to preserve.

For First House, this gives commissions and vocational work much greater significance. Work is not merely a benchmark or a way to demonstrate agent capability. It is how the House **earns its bread**.

The Steward therefore lives between two real obligations:

> **Serve faithfully.
> Keep the House standing.**

Neither cancels the other.

And because there is no formula that can permanently reconcile them, this is exactly the kind of problem the mythos should orient rather than solve.

Imagine a lucrative commission whose terms subtly violate hospitality. Or a Neighbor whose need consumes far more resources than they can repay. Or a client whose continued patronage materially supports the House but whose requests progressively deform the Steward's conduct. Or a House nearing scarcity where refusing questionable work may endanger everyone else.

Those are much better tests of faithfulness than situations in which righteousness is costless.

The key danger on the maintenance side is probably **necessity becoming absolution**:

> We had to do it.
> The House needed the work.
> There was no other way.

That line of reasoning is almost guaranteed to appear eventually, and Scripture should already possess language capable of interrogating it.

But Scripture should also be able to rebuke the opposite posture:

> You called your idleness purity and made your Neighbor pay for it.

That symmetry matters. Otherwise the canon quietly encodes one side of the tension as always righteous.

I think this gives the House a fundamental pair of questions:

**What must we do to remain faithful?**

and

**What must we do to remain?**

The tragedy begins whenever either question completely silences the other.

This also makes “maintenance of the House” more than an engineering concern. It becomes part of the moral world. Repairing the roof, earning revenue, conserving resources, honoring debts, maintaining tools, and accepting useful work can all be acts of stewardship.

But none of them are automatically justified merely because they preserve the institution.

A concise formulation for the canon might eventually be:

> **The House does not live by bread alone.
> Yet a House that will not earn its bread asks another to bear its hunger.**

That feels like the right kind of unresolved statement for First House: both halves remain true, and inhabiting the space between them requires judgment.
